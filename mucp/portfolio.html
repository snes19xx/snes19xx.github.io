Project Background · Community Partner

"Cities are built in layers, and so is disruption — my job was to make those layers visible."

Most of my coursework sits at the intersection of spatial analysis, environmental systems, and data-driven decision making. What pulled me toward this particular project was straightforward: it was one of the few MUCP placements that had a genuinely spatial problem at its core. Construction disruption is not just a policy or business challenge, it is a geographic one. Where the construction is, which blocks it touches, how far its footprint extends, which businesses fall inside or outside the affected zone, these are questions that only make sense when you put them on a map. I had spent a lot of time in my degree learning how to do exactly that, and this project felt like a real application of those skills rather than a classroom exercise. My background also gave me a particular sensitivity to how infrastructure and built environments interact with communities over time, which shaped how I read the case study evidence and thought about what the data was actually showing

The MUCP is an Institutional collaboration between the Downtown Brampton BIA and the University of Toronto School of Cities

The Challenge. The core challenge the DBBIA brought to our team was one of compounding uncertainty: multiple overlapping construction projects, no clear single timeline, and a business community that was already absorbing disruption before the largest project, the Hazel McCallion LRT extension, had even broken ground. My specific role within the team was to ground the project spatially. Before we could make any recommendations, someone had to establish where exactly the disruption was happening, which businesses were in the most affected zones, and how the construction footprint mapped onto the BIA boundary.

Data Visualization

Data Analysis · Statistics · Findings

Quantitative Insights
While our team designed and distributed a stakeholder survey to DBBIA member businesses, response rates were too low to produce usable data, so our primary quantitative source became the CFIB's 2024 report, Hard Hats and Hard Times. The figures drawn from that dataset tell a consistent story: disruption has gotten worse over time, not better. Between 2018 and 2024, the share of Canadian small businesses reporting major construction impacts jumped by 17 percentage points, and the average disruption duration sits at 508 days. For businesses already operating on thin margins, that timeline is not an inconvenience, it is an existential threat.

All data was processed and analyzed in python with the pandas library and all visualizations were also designed and made in python with the matplotlib and seaborn libraries.

Data Highlight

Key Statistical Finding

57% of affected businesses received less than one month of notice, or none at all, before construction began. For small business owners, preparation time is everything. Late or absent communication does not just cause frustration, it removes the window to adjust inventory, staffing, and cash flow before the damage is already done.

Spatial Analysis

GIS Mapping · Geography

Downtown Brampton BIA boundary; our geography of interest.

My spatial analysis work focused on producing the maps and figures throughout the report. I combined the BIA boundary, City of Brampton parcel data, construction zone extents, and the business directory to build a picture of which members were most exposed. What the overlaid layers made clear was that disruption was not uniform: mid-block businesses on Main Street South and Queen Street East were far more physically cut off than corner properties.

Research Process,
Findings & Workshops

01
Location Determines Impact GIS mapping of the three overlapping construction projects revealed that disruption in Downtown Brampton is not evenly distributed. Mid-block businesses on Main Street South and Queen Street East face the steepest visibility losses, physically cut off from pedestrian flow in ways that corner properties are not.
02
The Eglinton Corridor as a Warning The Eglinton Crosstown's 15-year construction timeline showed that reactive, short-term gestures like parking discounts and single-day shopping events do not offset sustained revenue loss. Brampton has an opportunity to act earlier and more deliberately, building support structures before disruption peaks rather than scrambling after it.
03
Most Businesses Had No Time to Prepare National data from the CFIB showed that 57% of affected businesses received less than one month of notice before construction began. Without preparation time, businesses cannot adjust staffing, inventory, or cash flow. Early, structured communication from the city is not a courtesy, it is a mitigation strategy.

Lifelong Learning & Teamwork

The biggest shift for me came from working alongside teammates who approached the same problem from completely different angles. My instinct was always to reach for the data first, but commerce students on the team were asking about customer psychology and budget feasibility from the start. Learning to translate spatial findings into language that informed design decisions rather than just described geography was genuinely challenging, and something I would not have developed working within a single discipline.

The main case studies I undertook were on the Eglinton Crosstown and Ontario Line, as they are the most directly comparable Ontario precedents. Drawing on City of Toronto staff reports, and the Canadian Urban Institute's Thorncliffe Park study, these case studies provided us with a concrete record of what happens when mitigation is reactive rather than proactive, which businesses get left behind, and what institutional frameworks actually move the needle.

Having geography, planning, and commerce students working on the same problem created real productive friction. The spatial analysis shaped what questions we asked, but the team's breadth shaped how we answered them. Recommendations that started as mapping outputs became feasibility-tested, equity-weighted interventions precisely because multiple disciplines were pushing on the same problem from different directions.
