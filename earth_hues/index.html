Earth in Hues

Computing the true area-weighted spectral signature of Earth's surface from multi-source satellite data. Categorized by land cover and calendar month.

1. Overview 2. Data Sources 3. Methodology · 3.1 Grid 3.2 GEBCO 3.3 Land Cover 3.4 Weighting 3.5 Masks 3.6 Extraction · 4. Visualizations · 4.1 Color Grid 4.2 Signature 4.3 Drift 4.4 Wheels 4.5 RGB 4.6 Brightness Δ · 5. Notes

1. Overview

The project processes multi-source satellite data to compute the true, physically-representative average RGB color of distinct surface categories: oceans, forests, deserts, ice sheets, urban areas, and more, for each calendar month. The result is a structured dataset (`earth_hues.json`) mapping every surface category to a hex color per month, corrected for the spatial distortion inherent in geographic coordinate grids. The output is then used in D3 for visualizations.

2. Data Sources

Three datasets:

Dataset Format Description
NASA Blue Marble (×12) GeoTIFF True-colour RGB surface reflectance composites at ~10 km resolution, one per calendar month
GEBCO 2025 GeoTIFF tiles Global seabed and terrain elevation, stitched from a multi-tile mosaic
MODIS MCD12C1 HDF IGBP land cover classification at 0.05° (~5.6 km per pixel), 17 discrete classes

The Blue Marble composites provide the raw colour signal. GEBCO provides the elevation dimension needed to split ocean from inland water and to define the mountain mask. MODIS provides the categorical overlay that separates forests from grasslands, croplands from deserts, and so on.

3. Methodology

3.1 Reference Grid Establishment

All datasets are aligned to a common reference frame derived from the January Blue Marble composite: a global unprojected grid in EPSG:4326 (WGS84) with uniform 0.1° spacing.

$$\text{Grid} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}, \quad \Delta\lambda = \Delta\phi = 0.1°$$

This covers $[-90°, 90°] \times [-180°, 180°]$. Every subsequent dataset is aligned to this transform before any computation.

3.2 GEBCO Elevation Mosaic

GEBCO 2025 distributes global bathymetry as regional tiles at ~450 m native resolution. Each tile's spatial bounds are located in the reference frame using rasterio's window transform:

$$W = \text{from\_bounds}(\text{bounds}_{\text{tile}},\ T_{\text{ref}})$$

Each tile is downsampled from disk via average resampling:

$$\hat{e}_{i,j} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{i,j}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{i,j}} e_p$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}$ stores signed elevation in metres — negative values below sea level.

3.3 Land Cover Reprojection

The MODIS MCD12C1 HDF encodes IGBP land surface classification as subdataset 0. The file lacks a standard geotransform, so reprojection is handled explicitly with source and destination transforms provided manually. Nearest-neighbour resampling is required — the class labels are discrete integers and interpolation would produce meaningless fractional values.

$$L_{i,j} = L_{\text{src}}\!\left(T_{\text{src}}^{-1}\!\left(T_{\text{ref}}(i, j)\right)\right)$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{Z}^{1800 \times 3600}$ assigns each pixel one of 17 IGBP classes (0–16).

3.4 Latitude-Cosine Area Weighting

Motivation

In an equirectangular grid every pixel covers $\Delta\phi \times \Delta\lambda$ degrees, but the physical area shrinks dramatically toward the poles. Without correction, polar regions are massively overrepresented in a simple mean.

Derivation

Treating the Earth as a sphere of radius $R$, the circumference at latitude $\phi$ is:

$$C(\phi) = 2\pi R \cos(\phi)$$

A longitude step $\Delta\lambda$ therefore spans physical width $R\cos(\phi)\,\Delta\lambda$, and pixel area is:

$$A(\phi) = R^2 \Delta\phi\,\Delta\lambda \cos(\phi)$$

Since $R^2\Delta\phi\,\Delta\lambda$ is constant, the relative area weight is simply:

$$\boxed{w(\phi) = \cos(\phi)}$$

Implementation

$$\phi_i = T_{\text{ref}}(i,\ 0)_y \qquad w_i = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi \phi_i}{180}\right)$$
Location Latitude $\phi$ Weight $w(\phi)$
Equator $0°$ $1.000$
New York $40.7°$ $\approx 0.758$
Oslo $59.9°$ $\approx 0.502$
North Pole $90°$ $0.000$

3.5 Surface Category Masking

The mountain category requires a topographic roughness index from the DEM gradient, distinguishing rugged terrain from flat elevated plateaus:

$$\rho_{i,j} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\right)^2}$$

3.6 Area-Weighted Mean Color Extraction

For each month $m$ and category $k$, the working pixel set intersects category mask with valid-data mask:

$$\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)} = \text{mask}_k \;\cap\; \text{valid}_m$$

The area-weighted mean for each channel $c \in \{R, G, B\}$:

$$\bar{c}_k^{(m)} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}\; c_{i,j}}{\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}}$$

Channel means are rounded, clamped to $[0,255]$, and encoded as hex:

$$\text{hex}_k^{(m)} = \texttt{\#}\,\lfloor \bar{R} \rceil_{255}\,\lfloor \bar{G} \rceil_{255}\,\lfloor \bar{B} \rceil_{255}$$

4. Visualizations

Six charts explore the dataset from different angles: from a raw colour grid to channel-level decompositions and seasonal brightness anomalies. There are more visualizations in the observable notebook.

4.1 True Mean Earth Color

Each cell shows the area-weighted mean hex colour for a surface type and month. Hover any cell for values.

Findings

The ocean row is immediately legible: twelve near-identical squares of very deep navy (#030614), confirming that total ocean mean luminance is essentially constant. The ocean absorbs nearly all incident visible radiation at every wavelength, and month-to-month variance from phytoplankton or surface roughness is negligible against that deep reflectance floor.

Snow and Ice shows the opposite extreme — consistently the brightest row, near-white all year, with a barely perceptible warm shift in Northern Hemisphere summer as Arctic melt ponds and older snow reduce overall albedo. The Forests row traces a clear arc: warm dark-olive in boreal winter, deepening to near-black (#1b290d) in July–September, then recovering — the tropical evergreen core damps the signal but does not eliminate the deciduous pulse.

4.2 Earth's Annual Color Signature

The three aggregate signals (total Earth, total land, and total ocean) across the full year.

Findings

Carl Sagan famously described Earth as a "pale blue dot" Cand it holds at interplanetary distance, where atmospheric scattering dominates the signal. The surface data differs. The Total Earth Mean sits around #242528 in summer and #28292d in winter — a near-neutral very dark grey with only a marginal blue bias from the ocean's B channel. The ocean absorbs roughly 94–98% of incident visible light across all wavelengths, and the land surface average is dominated by soil, dry vegetation, and forest canopy, all of which are dark and low-chroma. Area-weighted, Earth's surface is not pale and not meaningfully blue. The pale blue dot is an atmospheric and geometric effect; the surface mean is just dark.

4.3 Seasonal Color Drift from January

RGB Euclidean distance of each month's mean colour from the January baseline, per surface type.

Findings

Wetlands dominates by a wide margin, exceeding 200 RGB units by August–September — the largest seasonal excursion of any land surface class. This is a bimodal signal: boreal winter produces cool grey-green tones from frozen Arctic peatland pixels, while Northern Hemisphere summer brings dramatic darkening as vegetation growth and open water reflectance take over.

Shrublands shows the second-largest drift (~130 units at peak), building slowly through June then spiking sharply into July–September. This is the double-hemisphere drying signal: Southern Hemisphere winter (Australian spinifex, Karoo, Patagonian steppe) coincides with Northern Hemisphere summer drought (Mediterranean maquis, California chaparral), both pushing toward dark brown at the same time. Deserts and Snow/Ice are near-flat throughout — spectrally stable surfaces with minimal seasonal change.

4.4 Seasonal Color Wheels

Each biome as a 12-segment wheel. The outer rim marks the four seasons; inner wedges show mean colour per month, starting January at 12 o'clock.

Findings

The Oceans wheel is a near-perfect monochrome disc. Snow and Ice is equally static but bright. Forests and Wetlands show the most visually striking wheels: a clear split between the lighter boreal-winter half and the darker summer half, with the transition aligning well with the seasonal rim boundaries. The Shrublands wheel captures the mid-year darkening neatly — summer wedges are visibly darker against the warmer winter half.

4.5 RGB Channel Decomposition

R, G, and B channel intensities (0–255) per surface type across twelve months. Separating channels reveals what drives visible colour changes.

Findings

For most land categories all three channels track together — seasonal change is primarily a brightness modulation, not a hue shift. Shrublands is the clearest exception: in July–August all three channels drop sharply, but red and green fall more steeply than blue, giving the summer minimum its warm brown character. This is consistent with dry senesced vegetation: chlorophyll absorption drops, but residual lignin and soil contribute elevated red relative to blue.

The Oceans panel shows three near-flat lines at very low values — R ≈ 3, G ≈ 6, B ≈ 20 — confirming the deep navy and the slight blue dominance across the ocean's reflectance floor. Snow and Ice shows all three channels tightly clustered near 234–235 with only the faintest warm shift in summer months.

4.6 Seasonal Brightness Anomaly

Deviation of monthly perceptual luminance from the annual mean for each surface type. Red = brighter than average; green = darker.

Findings

Wetlands commands the heatmap: January–May run strongly positive (+16 to +18 pp), while August–October plunge to −27 to −29 pp — by far the widest annual luminance swing. Shrublands shows the second-sharpest contrast, with its darkest cells (−18) in August–September standing against the warm positive values in winter. The asymmetry is notable: winter values (+7) are modest against summer lows, reflecting that the double-hemisphere darkening is an active compounding effect, not simple seasonal rotation.

Grasslands and Croplands show similar shapes, positive January–April, negative July–October which is consistent with Northern Hemisphere agricultural cycles dominating both signals. The Urban Areas row is nearly flat throughout (+0 to +4 in winter, −0.4 to −2.6 in summer), confirming spectral stability as a real property of the MODIS urban class. Total Ocean Mean is a uniform row of near-zeros.

5. Notes & Limitations

Resampling strategy

Average resampling preserves radiometric accuracy for continuous GEBCO elevation. Nearest-neighbour is reserved for categorical MODIS land cover to prevent class interpolation artifacts.

Polar weighting

Cosine weighting suppresses polar pixels toward zero without hard-excluding them. Contribution diminishes smoothly as $\phi \to \pm 90°$.

Mountain definition

The mountain mask requires elevation above 1000 m and roughness $\rho > 50$, excluding flat elevated plateaus. It is non-exclusive — pixels may belong simultaneously to Mountains and another category such as Shrublands.

Urban Areas anomaly

The olive-brown urban signature is a real result, not an artifact. At MODIS 0.05° resolution no pixel resolves a pure urban surface. Additionally, the IGBP classifier was trained predominantly on North American and European reference data and systematically misclassifies dense South and Southeast Asian cities, which are more often labelled as cropland or shrubland. The urban class therefore describes low-density suburban fabric, not global urban land as experienced by most city-residents.

Cloud contamination

MODIS composites include cloud-screening, but residual effects are more prevalent in persistently cloudy regions (Congo Basin, Maritime Southeast Asia, Amazon), which may be systematically underrepresented.

Colour space

All averages are computed on gamma-compressed values as stored in the GeoTIFFs, not on physically linear light. The perceptual difference is small for broad-average statistics of this kind.
